How the claim was read — the 6 parts we had to pin down first

A claim like this can only be checked once it is broken into the things it asserts. That parse decides which figures are even the right ones to look at, and it happens before any search.

What it asserts
Net migration fell from about 135,000 to about 24,000
Who is speaking
Erica Stanford, in her role as Immigration Minister
The subject
Immigration, read as a claim about the government's own performance
The period
“Since I got the job” → read as November 2023 to the latest figures (the date was not stated — we took the month she took office)
The numbers
Annual net migration, in people, as a level — not a percentage or a rate
The series it leans on
Stats NZ's published net migration estimates
Assumed, not said
That the fall is because of the changes she made. The sentence is also the argument for them — and that implied cause is the part we ended up testing.

One judgement call is worth naming: “since I got the job” has no date in it. We read it as the month she became Minister rather than choosing the window that best suited either side, and the alternative framings below show what happens if you read it differently.

What we did not check, and why — the 5 sentences set aside

Every sentence in the interview was classified. Five could not be settled by evidence, each for a specific reason:

  • “It's not fair on New Zealanders who are doing it tough.” — opinion: a judgement, not a fact about the world.
  • “This is the most significant reset in a generation.” — rhetoric: a superlative with no agreed measure.
  • “I'll take the question on notice.” — procedure: about the interview, not the subject.
  • “Isn't that what you promised in 2023?” (the host) — question: the answer to it is checked on the other claim's page.
  • “We're committed to getting the settings right.” — a commitment: checkable only when a deadline passes, so it is held rather than graded.

This list is the honest edge of the verdict: what you are reading is a finding about two sentences, not about the interview.

The claim was classified

It states a number over a period, so it was sent to the official figures. That choice was made from the claim's wording alone — before any figures were fetched, and before we knew what they would say.

Decision
Kind of claim: a statistic. Kind of check: the official series.
Not a quotation check (nothing is being quoted), not an open-web search (an official series covers this number exactly), not a context check (the claim's context is the statement itself).
The four other checks available, and why this one

Read the source it cites — no source is named in the sentence, so there is nothing to read.

Compare it to the recording — only for claims about what someone said; this claim is about migration, not about the interview.

Check the context it carries — used when a claim is true but attached to the wrong event, document or time. The implied cause here is closer to this, and the figures test it directly instead.

Search the open web — the fallback when no official series covers the number. It is our least reliable check, so it is only used when there is no series. (Here: 1 of the 2 claims took this route — the 2023 commitment has no series behind it.)

Recording this matters because it is the one decision that cannot be made after seeing the answer: picking the check that agrees with the claim is how a fact-check becomes a press release.

The figures were compared — six ways

The claim's own window checks out: net migration did fall from 135,000 to about 24,000. So the rest of the check is about the part the sentence does not say — whether that fall is because of the changes, or whether it was already happening.

Decision
Both numbers are accurate. Three of the six alternative framings contradict the implication that the changes caused the fall.
That combination is what makes this “accurate but incomplete” rather than “supported”: the arithmetic is not in dispute, the timing is.

The search, and what it threw away

Before any fetching, the claim was broken into the questions that evidence could actually answer — then searched for those, not for the claim. Four searches ran; one of the four was thrown out on sight.

The 3 questions, and what each search returned

Asked: what was annual net migration in the year to November 2023? Returned the official series. Kept.

Asked: when did the current downward trend begin? Returned the same series plus one newsroom summary. Kept, and this is the question that produced the material rows above.

Asked: did the 2023 changes cause the fall? Returned commentary, one advocacy release and one paywalled op-ed. Nothing usable — see below.

The third question is the one the claim's implication needs, and it is the one the evidence could not answer. That is recorded here rather than quietly dropped, because it is the reason the verdict stops where it does.

What the second pass caught, and how the verdict was reached

Passed on 3 of 4 statements. One draft sentence — “the fall is the largest on record” — was rejected: the figures did not state it and we had added it ourselves. It was cut before publication, not after.

What tipped it from “supported” to “accurate but incomplete”: row 3 of the framing table, where the fall is already underway in the year before the changes. The claim is true of the period it names and misleading about the period it implies.

Nothing has changed since — this is version 1 of the verdict, first published 10:12 am on 9 Sept.

What this check could not settle
  • Whether the fall continues. The series stops at June 2026; anything after that is not in the record yet.
  • What the policy itself did, separately from the trend that was already running. The figures show timing, not cause — and no published evaluation separates the two.
  • The 2023 commitment. Tracked on its own page, because the coalition agreement is evidence that has not been published in a form we can read.
