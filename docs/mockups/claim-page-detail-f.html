02What is this claim actually made of?9 Sept · 9:12 am

Before looking anything up, the sentence was broken into the parts it asserts: the number, the period, the series it leans on, and who is speaking. Only then can the right figures be picked — a claim about net migration over a period is checked against a different series than one about arrivals per capita.

One part of this claim is not stated at all: “since I got the job” implies the fall is because of the changes she made. That implied cause is what the check ended up turning on, and naming it here is what made it testable.

The period also had no date in it. We read it as November 2023, the month she took office — and step 04 shows what changes if you read it from the peak of the trend instead.

03Which kind of check does this claim get?9 Sept · 9:14 am

A number over a period that an official series covers → the official figures. Chosen from the claim's wording alone, before any figures were seen.

Four other checks exist and were ruled out: reading a cited source (none is named), comparing against the recording (the claim is about migration, not about what was said), checking the context the claim carries (the figures test the implied cause more directly), and searching the open web (only for claims with no official series — our least reliable check).

Recording the choice matters because it is the one decision that cannot be made honestly after the answer is known.

04Does the claim hold under every fair reading of the same figures?9 Sept · 9:20–9:35 am

The claim's own window checks out: net migration did fall from 135,000 to about 24,000. So the check moved to the part the sentence does not say. The same series was cut six ways, and three of the six contradict the implied cause:

The fall had already begun in the year before the changes took effect. It is a fall in arrivals, not a rise in departures — not the story of people leaving. And the size depends on the start date: 82% from her first month, 75% from the peak of the trend.

None of that makes the numbers wrong. It makes the reason they are being told unsupported.

05What did the search find — and what was thrown away?9 Sept · 9:26 am

The claim was broken into the questions evidence could answer, and searched for those rather than for the claim. Three questions, four searches, two rounds of a cap of three.

Answered: what was net migration in the year to Nov 2023. Answered, and decisive: when did the trend begin — this produced the row the verdict turns on. Not answered: did the changes cause the fall. That question is the one the claim's argument needs; nothing published settles it, so the verdict stops at “incomplete” rather than reaching for “false”.

One source found by the search was discarded on sight: a campaign group's release restating the claim. An advocacy body's framing is not the record, and it adds nothing to a check that has the official series.

Appendix · what this check could not settle, and what would change it

Unsettled

  • Whether the fall continues. The series ends at June 2026; nothing after that is in the record.
  • What the policy itself did, separately from the trend already running. Timing is not cause, and no published evaluation separates them.
  • The 2023 commitment from the same interview. Tracked on its own page.

Would change this verdict

  • An evaluation attributing the fall to the 2023 changes — moving the implied cause to supported, or away from it.
  • A revised vintage showing the trend began after November 2023 — removing the row this verdict turns on.
  • A statement of the period meant by “since I got the job” — the framing table is recomputed against it.
